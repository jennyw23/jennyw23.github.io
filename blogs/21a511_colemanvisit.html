A Visit from Anthropologist, Gabriella Coleman

March 30, 2021

Today’s discussion with Gabriella Coleman unpacked many of my questions regarding how our current social and political landscape has affected the hacker communities she comprehensively documented. Coleman’s responses opened up new questions that I hadn’t considered earlier. Throughout both Coding Freedom and Hacking Diversity, I found it very interesting to learn about how diversity has and has not worked its way into hacker culture. In Coleman’s “Craft and Craftiness” Chapter, she discussed in great detail that meritocracy is one of the most important values of this community. However, I did not consider how meritocracy could serve as a legitimate hindrance to creating a diverse and open space until Coleman brought up the case of Richard M. Stallman.

After reading more about Stallman’s reinstatement to the Free Software Foundation (FSF) board, I found a tweet in this article. that made me consider a new question, as well as see an unexpected parallel between F/OSS and the art/music industry. The tweet, voiced by a former FSF board member, stated “The idea that someone who does enough "good work" earns a pass for inappropriate behavior is pervasive, and fosters environments where abusers can prosper. People who hold this belief shouldn't be involved in running organizations."

How might a meritocratic system cloud our judgment when it comes to judging bigoted but intelligent people? How have we already seen this come to play more prominently in music (should we be able to separate the music from the artist? e.g. XXXTentacion, Kanye West, 6IX9INE)? And bringing the question back to the issue of meritocracy, how might an inability to separate merit from bigotry from within the community itself hinder productive social change?

Something I found thought-provoking during Coleman and Professor Beltran’s exchange regarding diversity advocacy institutionally was the talk of “trendiness”. Coleman brought up the question of “at what point do things go beyond the trend? When the trend ends, how does the issue maintain its importance?” Currently, diversity and inclusion is trendy. Companies emphasize a welcoming culture. Universities highlight their POCs on their advertising campaigns. Debian has created ‘Diversity Teams’. In regards to companies and universities, I expect that once the trend ends, diversity/inclusion won’t necessarily go away. There are prominent figures who will not rest until their needs are heard.

However, I wonder about F/OSS. Coleman stated that open source projects––even now––don’t necessarily have the resources or buy-in to make social changes. The emphasis on meritocracy within these communities makes it even harder to separate people’s damaging actions from their intellect. Richard Stallman’s reinstatement is not only a clear indicator of this, but also a tangible example that this is a real problem. Another tweet from the article emphasized this, explaining, "The thing that randos who have never had to actually work with RMS [Richard M. Stallman] don't understand is that MANY people who deeply respected him tried to help him learn to not objectify women, shout over others at Libreplanet as if it was his birthday party, stop sh*t like 'emacs virgins." Instead, "That energy, utterly wasted, could've been spent advocating for free software and building the inclusive, impractical community so many people WANT. Spent, instead, on a man's ego, over and over."

After this discussion, I am now even more wary that meritocracy is a flawed social system. I still think it’s incredibly important to reward hard work and ability (I have always believed that meritocracy is the most unbiased system), but this course has made me question that claim quite substantially…
