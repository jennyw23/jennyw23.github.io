A Formal Analysis on the Facade of Clapp Library

February 26, 2022 Art History, Wellesley College by admin
View In PDF Form

The Facade of Clapp Library was part of Andrew Carnegie’s 1905 commission to establish a centerpiece academic building for Wellesley College. Although the library was completed in 1909-1910, its side and rear exteriors underwent renovations in 1956-1959 and 1973-1975. Since its initial completion, only two additions were made to the facade: a statue of Athena installed in June 1912 and a statue of Hestia installed in June 1913.

The center of the facade consists of a set of bronze doors carved with two figures topped by an intricate carving of the Wellesley College seal and motto. Two columns of the Ionic order stand adjacent to the doors, emphasizing the doors’ role as the focal point of the facade. A carved pattern consisting of circles with round flourishes surrounds the doors, serving as an ornate ledge for the akroterion-like Wellesley College seal. Panning outward, the entryway is flanked by two bronze statues raised on concrete bases.

The facade is in excellent condition. There are no cracks in the panels, nor signs of aging in the columns. The acanthus leaf ornaments and other decorative pieces remain intact and untarnished. The bronze statues show slight corrosion from environmental factors, and the statue of Athena on the right has since lost its arms. However, both figures’ details and contrapposto poses are still clearly visible.

The facade’s base is characterized by strong right angles, which highlight the structure’s stability and direction as an entryway towards academic focus. At the center, the doors are framed by a rectangular border and lined by smooth orthogonal panels. To the sides, both statues stand in front of a recessed wall niche of similar shape and proportion to the doors. The niche is also reinforced by a rectangular border. Looking at the facade as a whole, this repeated use of perpendicular lines accentuates its symmetry along its vertical axis.

While the designers relied on linear forms to structure the functional elements of the exterior, they employed curves and natural flows through the decorative elements. The circle pattern around the door’s border resembles a repetition similar to that of a meander in Greek architecture, drawing the viewer’s eyes toward the bronze doors. Upon closer inspection, two decorative borders of flowers and leaves draw the focus even narrower to the figures, as well as to the label below them. On the left, there is a man holding a book with the word “sapientia” below him. On the right, a woman holds a child and has the word “caritas” below her. Above the figures, the same leafy border is used to stress the College motto, “non ministrare, sed ministrare”. If we look away from the doors and focus on the entire exterior, we also notice that the wall niches and decorative pieces behind the statues serve as tools which help frame the figures.

The architect implements various border designs within the doors, surrounding the doors, and behind the statues. The decorations are deliberately placed around the facade to present different motifs on an otherwise plain surface. By lining the orthogonal structures, the decorative borders’ positioning suggest that they are meant to support and spotlight the main symbols––the figures––rather than eclipse them. Furthermore, the akroterion seal above the doors unifies each component of the facade, creating elegance by garnishing the geometric symmetry.

The facade is built using symmetry and sharp angles in order to highlight the major symbols. When we look at the facade, the bronze doors, statues, and college seal stand out as unique aspects of the work. Our brains use the symmetry of the identical-sized doors, identical wall niches, and identical columns to organize and process the visuals. When focusing on the statues, the symmetric view helps the brain focus on the left, then use its understanding of reflection to scan to the right. When looking at the doors, the symmetry of the panels helps the eyes move from left to right without getting lost in details. Consequently, the architects were able to employ this orthogonal symmetry to juxtapose and bring together symbols of learning and love through the statues.

The statue of Athena, goddess of wisdom, and carving of the man carrying a book, the sapientia figure, symbolize the drive for intellectual pursuits. Contrastingly, the statue of Hestia, goddess of the hearth, and carving of the woman holding a child, the caritas figure, symbolize the responsibility to nurture and love. Notably, each statue is paired with a carving of opposite symbolism. To the left, we see the statue of Hestia (home) and carving of the intellect (wisdom). To the right, we see the statue of Athena (wisdom) and carving of the nurturing mother (home). This choice of design suggests that the facade is meant to encapsulate the intersection of wisdom and home, rather than the union. Individuals who walk through the entryway are not to be intellectuals at times and nurturers at others. Rather, they are meant to embody both qualities, understanding their roles as academics and nurturers at all times.
